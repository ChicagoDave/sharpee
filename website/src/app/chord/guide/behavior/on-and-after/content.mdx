Behavior attaches to its owner as an `on` or `after` clause inside the
owner's `create` block (or inside a trait). The two words divide
one moment: `on` runs before the action commits and may stop it; `after`
runs once it has happened and reacts.

```chord
create the hive box
  aka hive, box
  a thing, scenery, openable
  in the Apiary
  states: humming, quiet

  A white wooden hive box, lid held down by a brick.

  on the player opening
    refuse when the hive box is humming: bees-disturbed
    change the hive box to quiet
  end on

  after the player opening
    phrase honey-smell
  end after
```

The division is enforced: `refuse`, `refuse when`, and `must` are parse
errors inside an `after` body (`parse.react-refusal`). If a reaction
wants to stop something, it is not a reaction; move it to `on`. Every
clause closes with an `end` line matching its opener.

## The head names who acts

A clause head is *actor, then verb*: `on the player opening`. The block it
sits in is what is acted **on** — the hive box above — so the head has one
job, naming who is doing it.

```chord
create Marguerite
  a person
  starts in the Counting House

  The senior clerk, and she knows it.

create the ledger
  a thing
  in the Counting House

  A ledger, spine cracked.

  on the player taking
    refuse ledger-not-yours
  end on

  after Marguerite taking
    phrase she-checks-the-column
  end after
```

The two clauses sit on the same object and fire for different people. Write
the owner's name where you would once have written `it`.

## `the player` is a role, not a character

`the player` means whoever is the player character when the clause fires, so
a clause written with it follows the role if the story hands it to someone
else. To pin behavior to one particular character instead, write that
character's name.

## A character's own block needs no name

Inside a person's `create` block the subject is already settled, so the head
drops the actor — the same way `wears the boots` needs no subject.

```chord
create Marguerite
  a person
  starts in the Counting House

  The senior clerk, and she knows it.

  on going
    refuse she-will-not-leave-the-ledger
  end on
```

That bare head is her own going. Outside a person's block it is an error
(`analysis.head-bare-outside-actor`) — a hive box does not open things, so
`on opening` there has no one to mean.

## One head, several actions

When several actions get the same response, list them on one head with
commas. The head means one clause per action, each with the same actor,
condition, modifiers and body.

```chord
create the farmland view
  aka view, meadows, farms
  a thing, scenery
  in the Top of the Post

  The view is spectacular, but you don't have time to waste gawking.

  on the player taking, touching, pushing, pulling, climbing, searching
    refuse view-too-far
  end on
```

The list works on `after` heads and bare heads too, and anything after it
applies to every action listed: `on taking, dropping while the shop is
closed, once`. Commas are the only separator; `taking, touching or
pushing` is an error that shows the comma form, and an action listed twice
on one head is an error at the repeated word. A diagnostic about one of the
listed actions points at that word in the list.

## Verbs with nothing to act on

Some verbs have no object side, or need none: `jump`, `yes`, `no`, `sorry`,
`wave`. Each exists in the standard library with a stock line ("You jump on
the spot. Nothing comes of it."), and a clause speaks in its place. A verb
with no object is written on the **room** the player is in; a verb with one
goes in the thing's block as usual.

```chord
create the Landing Pad
  a room
  aka pad, pad 14

  Pad 14 is a circle of old cement under a black sky.

  on the player jumping
    phrase pad-jump
  end on

create the pad master
  a person
  in the Landing Pad
  states: unconvinced, waved-through

  on the player saying_yes while the pad master is unconvinced
    phrase quint-takes-yes-as-the-card
  end on

  on the player saying_no, saying_sorry
    phrase quint-shrugs
  end on
```

`yes`, `no` and `sorry` go in the block of the person the player is talking
to — the clause fires while a conversation with them is live — or on a room,
for an answer given to nobody. Inside an open `define exchange`, `yes` and
`no` are the exchange's answer and never reach these clauses. Two-word
verbs are written with an underscore, as `switching_on` and `taking_off`
are: `saying_yes`, `saying_no`, `saying_sorry`, `looking_under`. `wave` on
its own consults the room; `wave <thing>` consults the thing, which must be
held. `look under <thing>` reveals the `concealed` things it holds, as
`search` does; `empty <thing>` spills a container onto the floor, or into
another container with `empty <thing> into <other>`, and both take a clause
in the container's block (`on the player looking_under`, `after the player
emptying`).

## A value on the head

`set <thing> to <value>` carries a word, and a `setting` head can name it:
`on the player setting to kepler` fires only when that is the value set,
and `after the player setting` with no tail fires for every value. The tail
is for `setting` alone (`on the player taking to x` is an error naming
`while <thing> is x` as the form for other verbs), and the word must be one
of the thing's states.

## The other noun on the head

Throwing, giving, showing, putting, inserting and asking for a thing each
involve two nouns, and a clause on either one fires. `on the player
throwing` in a bin's block fires for anything thrown at the bin; to say
*which* thing, name it at the end of the head. The clause then fires only
when that noun is the one in the command.

```chord
create the returns bin
  a container, scenery
  in the Sorting Room

  on the player throwing the dead letter
    move the dead letter to the returns bin
    phrase letter-returned
  end on

create the dead letter
  a thing
  in the returns bin

  on the player throwing at the sorting machine
    phrase machine-spits-letter
  end on
```

The tail is read from the end of the head. The last `the`, `a` or `an`
opens it, and a preposition just before that article (`at`, `for`,
`from`, `about`, `in`) goes with it; `at`, `for` and `from` open a tail
on their own when the name has no article (`at Tobias`), and `giving to
Tobias` works as you would read it. The preposition is there for
reading and changes nothing. A tail goes on a head with one action only,
names a thing in the story, and is never the block's own owner; each of
those is an error that says so. If a name has an article inside it (`the
king of the hill`), the tail splits at the inner one; refer to the thing
by an alias without the article.

There is no word in the body for the other noun: the tail is how a clause
names it, and then the body can name it too.

`ask <person> for <thing>` is its own action, `asking_for`, separate from
`ask <person> about <topic>`. Nothing is handed over unless a clause does
it; with none, the person "doesn't seem inclined to hand over" the thing.

```chord
create the pad master
  a person
  in the Landing Pad
  states: unconvinced, waved-through

  on the player asking_for the tram token
    refuse when the pad master is unconvinced: quint-no-token
    move the tram token to the player
    phrase quint-hands-token
  end on
```
