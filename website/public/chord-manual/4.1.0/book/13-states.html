13 States

Part II built a world that a player can walk through and pick up. Nothing in it remembers anything. The grille is across the stair because a line says so, and the lens is cracked because its description says so, and neither of those is a fact the story could act on.

A state is such a fact. It is a word that says what condition a thing is in, chosen from a short list that you write. This chapter gives states to the story and to five of its things. The next chapter makes them change.

13.1 Declaring states

The iron grille across the Bell Stair is either standing or it has been lifted. That is one line in its block.

create the iron grille
  aka grille
  a thing, scenery
  in the Bell Stair
  states: standing, lifted

  Wrought iron, floor to vault, with no lock, no latch and no hinges.
  It was not built. It was put there.

The line is the word states, a colon, and the names separated by commas. A state name is one word, or several joined by hyphens. Three rules come with the line.

  • A thing is always in exactly one of its states.
  • The first state listed is the one it starts in. The grille starts standing.
  • The list has a direction. A thing may move to a later state and may not come back to an earlier one, unless you say otherwise.

The last rule is the one that surprises people, and it is deliberate. Most things in a story happen once. A seal is broken, a page is read, a bridge falls. Writing the states in order tells the compiler which way the story runs, and it will stop you if a later line tries to run it backwards.

Here are the other things in The Relighting that get states in this chapter.

create the lens
  a thing
  in the crate
  states: cracked, sound
create the cistern
  aka ice
  a thing, scenery
  in the Undercroft
  states: frozen, thawing, running

  A stone cistern sunk in the floor, fed by a lead pipe from somewhere
  above. It is a block of grey ice to its brim, and something flat and
  pale is held in the ice a hand's depth down.
create the tower bell
  aka bell
  a thing, scenery
  in the Bell Stair
  states: unrung, tolling, silent

  You cannot see the bell from here, only the dark it hangs in.
create the spark page
  aka first page, leaf
  a thing, readable with text "One line, in ink that is still wet. It is a single word, and it will not stay in your head."
  on the reading desk
  states: legible, learned

  A leaf that has worked loose from the grimoire. The ink on it is
  complete.

The cistern, the bell and the page are new. Each list tells a small story in order: the ice thaws and then runs, the bell has never rung, then tolls, then falls silent, and the page can be read before it has been learned.

13.2 Naming a state

A state names what the thing is. It does not name what the thing has stopped being. If you give a wax seal the states sealed, unsealed, the compiler refuses the second word with analysis.negated-state, because unsealed describes the absence of the first state and nothing else. Ask what the seal is once it is no longer sealed. It is broken, or melted, or lifted, and any of those tells the player and the next author more than the negative does.

13.3 Reading a state

A state is read with the word is. The phrase the iron grille is standing is a condition, which is to say something the story can test and find true or false. Conditions have a chapter of their own, which is chapter 16, but you met one place that takes a condition in chapter 12. A blocked way may be blocked only while something holds.

create the Bell Stair, a room
  in the Hall
  up to the Lantern Gallery
  up is blocked while the iron grille is standing: stair-sealed

In Part II the stair was sealed for good. Now it is sealed for as long as the grille is in its first state, and the line and the thing are tied together. To say that a thing is not in a state, write is not: the iron grille is not standing.

13.4 Changing a state

A state is changed with a statement, which is one line of instruction to the story.

change the iron grille to lifted

Statements cannot stand loose in a file. They live inside blocks that say when they run, and the block you will use most is the clause of chapter 14. There is one such block in the story already, the start block, and it will do for an experiment. The line needs the grille’s states line, so try it once this chapter’s story is in your folder, after the copy in “Build and try it” below. Add the line to the start block for a moment.

before the game starts
  change the player to Ansel
  change the iron grille to lifted
end before

Build the story and walk to the Bell Stair.

> up
Lantern Gallery
The roof of the tower, open on every side. The Great Lantern stands in the middle of it on a stone drum, taller than you are, its glass thirty years dirty. To the east a low parapet is all there is between you and the court. The stair goes back down.

The stair is open, because the condition on the blocked line is no longer true. Take the extra line out again before you go on. Keep the trick in mind, though. The start block is a good place to put the world into the state you want to test, without playing your way there each time.

If you ask for a state the thing does not have, the compiler tells you.

probe.story:27:3 error [analysis.undeclared-state] `melted` is not a declared state of seal.

13.5 States that go both ways

Nothing in Lanternhall goes back to what it was, which is rather the point of the story. For a thing that does, here is a sluice from another one.

create the sluice
  a thing, scenery
  in the Yard
  states, reversible: raised, lowered

  A sluice.

The word reversible goes after states and before the colon. With it, the sluice may be changed from raised to lowered and back as often as the story likes. Without it, the second change is an error, and the message shows you the line to write:

probe.story:37:3 error [analysis.irreversible-state] `whole-shell` is the initial state of a forward-only set — `change` cannot go back. Declare the set as `states, reversible: whole-shell, cracked-shell` to permit back-transitions.

Leave reversible off unless the thing really does go both ways. The forward-only rule will then catch the day you write a change in the wrong direction.

13.6 The story’s own states

Some facts belong to no one thing. Lanternhall as a whole is asleep, and then it is waking, and at the end it is awake. The story header takes a states line of the same shape.

story
  title: The Relighting
  id: relighting
  story-version: 0.3.0
  states: asleep, waking, awake

The story’s state is changed with change the story to waking, and it is read with the story is asleep or the story is not asleep. Everything said above about order and direction holds for the story as well.

Use the story’s states for the few large facts that many things will ask about. A fact about one thing belongs on that thing. Chord has no loose variables and no flags, and that is by design. Every fact has an owner, and you find the fact by looking at its owner’s block.

13.7 The states you do not declare

Some things already had states before this chapter. The library door is locked, the oak press is closed, and the hand lamp is unlit. Those come with the trait words of Part II, and they are read in the same way.

Trait word What you can read
openable is open, is closed
lockable is locked, is unlocked
switchable is on, is off
light-source is lit
wearable is worn
breakable is broken

So the library door is locked is a condition you may already write, and change the library door to unlocked is a statement you may already write. For light, say is lit and is not lit. The word unlit belongs to the starts unlit line and is not something a condition reads:

probe.story:26:42 error [analysis.unknown-value] `unlit` is not a state of lamp or a known trait — did you mean `lit`?.

Because the trait owns those words, you may not declare a state of the same name on a thing that has the trait.

probe.story:14:11 error [analysis.shadow-state] `open` is a word of `openable`, which box carries — a state of that name would answer `is open` in place of the trait. Rename the state, or read and change the trait's own (`is open`, `change … to open`).

One trait word from chapter 9 makes its positions out of your states. A thing that is settable is a dial, and the states you declare are the marks on it.

create the dial
  a thing, scenery, settable
  in the Yard
  states, reversible: low, high

  A dial with two marks, low and high.
> set dial to low
You set the dial to low.

> set dial to middling
The dial goes to low or high.

13.8 Build and try it

Copy relighting.story from the companion folder chapter-13 over your own file. It is the story with this chapter’s changes made. To make the changes by hand instead, work from this list and take the rest from that file: add the states line to the header and to the grille and the lens, add the cistern, the tower bell and the spark page, put the while condition on the Bell Stair’s blocked line, and raise story-version to 0.3.0, because this is the first chapter of Part III.

sharpee compose relighting.story --check
sharpee play .

Play it, and you will find that nothing has changed. That is correct. Every thing is in its first state and no line yet moves one. The page can be read, and reading it teaches nothing.

> read page
(first taking the spark page)

You take the spark page from the reading desk.

The spark page reads:
One line, in ink that is still wet. It is a single word, and it will not stay in your head.

Under the Hood: where a state lives · Story IR states

Compile the story with sharpee compose relighting.story -o relighting.json and look at the entry for the grille. Its states field holds the list in the order you wrote it, and a field beside it records whether the set is reversible. The trait states are not there. is locked and is lit are read from the platform’s own lockable and light-source traits each time they are asked for, which is why they can never disagree with what the standard actions have done.

From another system: properties and flags

In Inform 10 you would write “The cistern can be frozen, thawing or running” and then test and set that property anywhere in the source. In TADS 3 you would add a property to the object and assign to it from any method. Chord’s states line does the same job with two differences. The states are ordered, and the order is enforced unless you say reversible. And there is no global variable to fall back on: a fact belongs to a thing or to the story, and it is declared in that owner’s block.

13.9 What you have so far

The story and five of its things can now be in one condition or another, and one exit already depends on which. What is missing is anything that changes a state during play. For that a thing has to notice what the player does to it, and that is the next chapter.
