12 Ways that are closed, hidden or strange

An exit, so far, is either there or not there, and the only thing that has ever stood in one is a door. This chapter adds four more possibilities. A way can refuse the player with words of your choosing. It can kill them. It can exist only some of the time. And it can be a way that no compass direction describes, taken by typing a command of its own.

12.1 A way that refuses

The stair to the roof of Lanternhall has been sealed for thirty years by an iron grille that nobody built. The exit is there, and the player may not use it.

create the Bell Stair, a room
  in the Hall
  up to the Lantern Gallery
  up is blocked: stair-sealed

  The foot of the tower. A stair winds up around the well where the bell
  rope hangs, and an iron grille stands across its third step. The
  cloister is south.

The new line reads as English does: up is blocked, a colon, and a name. The name, stair-sealed, is a phrase key. It stands for a piece of text that is written elsewhere in the file, in a block near the end.

define phrases en-US
  stair-sealed:
    The grille stands across the stair, and there is no way round it.

This is the first phrase in The Relighting, and phrases have a whole chapter to themselves, which is chapter 20. What you need now is small. A define phrases block is a list of keys, each followed by a colon and then its text on the lines beneath, indented one level further. The words en-US say which language the text is in. A key is lower-case words joined by hyphens, and you make it up.

When the player tries the stair, the phrase is what they read.

> up
The grille stands across the stair, and there is no way round it.

You cannot write the text on the blocked line itself. What follows the colon is always a key. If the key has no phrase, the compiler says which one is missing:

relighting.story:102:3 error [analysis.missing-phrase] Phrase `stair-sealed` is not declared in en-US.

A blocked way wants something in the room to account for it, so the grille is a thing as well.

create the iron grille
  aka grille
  a thing, scenery
  in the Bell Stair

  Wrought iron, floor to vault, with no lock, no latch and no hinges.
  It was not built. It was put there.

The grille and the blocked line have nothing to do with each other as far as the story is concerned. The line stops the player, and the thing gives them something to look at. Keeping them in step is your job.

As it stands the stair is blocked for good, which makes the Lantern Gallery unreachable. A blocked line may carry a condition. It is written up is blocked while, then the condition, then the colon, and the way is blocked only while the condition holds. Conditions are the business of chapter 16, and lifting the grille is the business of chapter 24.

12.2 A way that kills

On the roof, a low parapet runs along the east side of the Gallery. There is no room to the east. There is a drop.

create the Lantern Gallery, a room
  in the Hall
  east is deadly: over-the-parapet
define phrases en-US
  over-the-parapet:
    You step up onto the parapet to see the court. The court comes up to
    meet you.

A deadly way needs no exit line, because it leads nowhere. When the player takes it, the phrase is printed and the story ends.

> east
You step up onto the parapet to see the court. The court comes up to meet you.

[The story has ended. RESTART, RESTORE or UNDO]

Be sparing with this, and be fair. The Gallery’s description now says, “To the east a low parapet is all there is between you and the court.” A player who has read that and walks east has been warned. A deadly way that the prose did not signal is a trap, and there is very little that players forgive less.

Because the stair is sealed, you cannot reach the parapet in the story as it stands. To see it work, wait until you have copied this chapter’s story into your folder, in “Build and try it” below. Then take out the up is blocked line for a moment, walk up and step off, and put the line back.

12.3 A way that is sometimes there

A blocked way is one the player can see and cannot take. A hidden way is not there at all until something makes it so. An exit line may end with while and a condition, and the exit then exists only while the condition is true.

create the Study, a room
  north to the Vault while the bookcase is open

Until the bookcase is open, going north from the Study gets the same answer as any direction with no exit. Nothing tells the player that a way exists. When the bookcase opens, the exit is simply there.

The difference between the two is what the player knows. Use blocked when they should see the obstacle and wonder how to get past it. Use a while exit when discovering that the way exists is itself the puzzle.

The Relighting has no hidden exit of this kind. It has something stranger.

12.4 A way with its own command

Behind the Refectory’s hearth, the iron plate does not sit square, and there is a gap a thin person could get through. It leads down into the Undercroft. It is not north or east or down. It is behind the hearth, and the player gets through it by saying so.

create the Refectory, a room
  in the Hall

  Three long tables, each with its row of candles, and a hearth you
  could stable a horse in. The benches are stacked against the wall,
  where someone left them for the holidays. The cloister is back to the
  west.

  passage to the Undercroft
    grammar
      squeeze or crawl behind [the] hearth
  end passage

A passage is an exit that is taken with a command instead of a direction. The block opens with passage to and the room it leads to, and it closes with end passage. Inside it, the word grammar stands on a line by itself, and the lines beneath that are the commands, indented one level further.

The command line is a pattern, and it has two small devices in it.

  • squeeze or crawl means that either word will do.
  • [the] means that the word in brackets may be typed or left out.

So this one line accepts squeeze behind hearth, crawl behind hearth, squeeze behind the hearth and crawl behind the hearth. Chapter 23 uses the same notation at length, for verbs of your own.

> examine hearth
The back of the hearth is a single iron plate, and it does not sit quite square. There is a hand's breadth of darkness down one side.

> squeeze behind hearth
It is pitch dark. You are likely to be eaten by a grue.

The Undercroft is dark, and Ansel has arrived in it without the lamp. That is the passage working as intended, and with a lit lamp in hand the same command shows the room.

Three properties of a passage are worth knowing.

A passage is one-way. The gap behind the hearth goes from the Refectory to the Undercroft, and there is no climbing back through it. If you want a way back, write a second passage in the other room.

Its commands are understood only in its own room. Type squeeze behind hearth in the Undercroft, where there is no such passage, and the story does not know the words:

> squeeze behind hearth
I don't understand that.

And nothing announces it. The player has to find the passage by reading. Here the hearth’s description does the work: “a hand’s breadth of darkness down one side” is an invitation, and squeeze is the verb it invites.

A passage may also be blocked or deadly, with a line inside the block: blocked: stair-sealed, or deadly: over-the-parapet, each naming a phrase key as before.

12.5 Build and try it

Copy relighting.story from the companion folder chapter-12 over your own file. It is the story with this chapter’s changes made. To make the changes by hand instead, work from this list and take the rest from that file: add the two lines to the Bell Stair and the Lantern Gallery, the passage to the Refectory, the grille, and the define phrases block. The phrases go after the last create block and before the start block. Nothing is raised in the header: this is still version 0.2.0, and it is the end of Part II.

sharpee compose relighting.story --check
sharpee play .

Fetch the lamp and light it, then go through the hearth.

> squeeze behind hearth
Undercroft
Low vaults on squat pillars, running off under the whole of the hall. The air is cold and smells of stone. The steps go back up.

You can see a crate here.

Under the Hood: reasons to refuse · if.action.going

All four of these are the same standard action, going, being given something to consult. A blocked line is a refusal with a message attached. A deadly line is a refusal that also ends the story. An exit with a while condition is an exit the action cannot find until the condition holds. A passage adds a command to the story’s grammar that leads to going, but only in the room that has the passage. In every case you have described the way, and the one action that moves the player has decided what to do about it.

From another system: unusual exits

In Inform 10 you would write a rule that stops the going action and prints a reason, and you would write a new action, with its own grammar line, for squeezing behind the hearth. In TADS 3 the direction property of a room can hold a message or a special connector object in place of a destination. Chord treats each of these as a fact about the way and gives each its own line. The gain is that the map remains readable as a map: every way out of a room, open or closed or strange, is in that room’s block. The cost is that you have these forms and no others, until Part III gives you clauses that can refuse anything at all.

12.6 What you have so far

That is the world. Lanternhall has eleven rooms in two regions, a locked door, a dark cellar, a sealed stair, a hidden way and a deadly one. It has a porter, a coat, a cake, a cup, a lamp, a lens in a crate and a book with one word in its margin. A player can walk it, pick it up, open it, wear it and unlock it.

What a player cannot do is change it. Nothing in Lanternhall yet reacts to anything. The rope rings no bell, the lens seats in the Lantern to no effect, and the lamp lights for anyone who asks. Part III is about making things happen.
