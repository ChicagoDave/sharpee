27 Asking and telling

The oldest way to talk to a character in interactive fiction is to ask about a subject: ask hobb about the bell. It has lasted because it is honest about what the game can do. The player names a subject, the character has something to say about it or has not, and nobody has to pretend the program understands English.

Chord’s form of it is a table. Each person has one, and each row of the table is a subject and what follows from raising it. This chapter writes the tables for Hobb and for Tamsin Vey, gives a book a table of its own, and then adds the two things that make a table sound like a person: a greeting and a manner.

27.1 A table of topics

define topics for Hobb
  about "dimming":
    phrase hobb-on-dimming
  about "lantern", "great lantern", "light":
    phrase hobb-on-lantern
  about "tamsin", "tamsin vey", "vey", "salvager":
    phrase hobb-on-tamsin
  about "gnusto":
    phrase hobb-on-gnusto
end topics

The block is a top-level declaration, like define action. It names the person it belongs to, and a person may have only one. Each row begins about, gives its subject, and ends with a colon. The body beneath is a list of statements, and a row with one statement may carry it on the same line.

A quoted subject is matched against what the player typed after about. The match is of the whole subject and is never a guess: “lantern” answers ask hobb about lantern and does not answer ask hobb about lanterns. Give a row every spelling a player might reasonably use, separated by commas.

> ask hobb about the dimming
He looks at the blue flame on the sill before he answers.

"Went out like a pipe," says Hobb. "No bang. The Master stood in the court with his hand up for an hour and nothing came to it."

> ask hobb about tamsin
He takes his time, of which he has had a great deal.

"Vey? Takes the brass and leaves the hinges. I let her. Brass doesn't keep the rain out." He considers. "Her word's good. It's all of her that is."

The sentence above each answer is one of three that Hobb’s manner takes in turn, which a later section of this chapter explains. Which of the three you see depends on how many answers he has given in your game, so in this chapter and the three after it, that sentence in your play will often be a different one of the three from the one the page shows.

Each answer has a sentence in front of it that is not in the phrase. That is Hobb’s manner, and this chapter comes to it below.

The player’s article is dropped before the match, so “dimming” answers both about dimming and about the dimming. For the same reason you write a subject once, without its article. Writing it both ways is an error.

probe.story:23:9 error [analysis.duplicate-topic] "rain" is already declared in this table (line 23) — aliases included, a topic answers in one place.

tell hobb about gnusto reaches the same table as ask hobb about gnusto. A subject has one row, and the row answers either verb.

27.1.1 A thing as a subject

A row may name a thing in the story in place of a quoted word.

about the kettle:
  phrase hobb-on-kettle
> ask hobb about the kettle
He turns the kettle a quarter round on its hook, to no purpose.

"Mine," says Hobb.

A row of this kind answers when the player’s words name that thing, by any of its names, and the thing is there to be named. The kettle is in the Lodge with Hobb, so the row works. It is the right form for something the player can point at.

It is the wrong form for a subject that is somewhere else. Tamsin Vey is in the Refectory, and a row written about Tamsin Vey in Hobb’s table would not answer a player standing in the Lodge. That is why Hobb’s row about her is quoted, with her names spelled out. People are asked about in their absence more often than not, so quote them.

A row cannot be both. If a table has a row for the thing, a quoted row spelling the thing’s name is an error.

probe.story:31:9 error [analysis.topic-entity-collision] "bucket" collides with `bucket` — that entity is already an entity-tier row of this table, and the quoted spelling would shadow its quiet entity resolution. Remove one.

27.1.2 What a row can do

A row’s body is statements, and they are the statements of chapter 15. A row can refuse, change a state, award a score, or have its owner act.

about "bell", "tolling", "noise":
  refuse when the tower bell is unrung: hobb-what-bell
  phrase hobb-on-bell
  Hobb gives the hush page to Ansel when Hobb has the hush page

This row is how the third working now reaches the player. Until this chapter the hush page lay on a shelf in the stacks. It has moved into Hobb’s keeping.

create Hobb
  a person, proper
  pronouns he
  in the Lodge
  carries the hush page
  mood calm

Ask him before the bell has rung, and the row refuses.

> ask hobb about the bell
"Bell hasn't rung in thirty years," says Hobb. "Ask me again when it does."

Ask him when the Hall is ringing with it, and he answers, and then acts.

> ask hobb about the bell
He takes one hand off one ear to hear you, and puts it back.

"It'll go on till it's told," Hobb shouts. "There's a word for the telling. I kept it by, in a newspaper, in case."

Hobb gives the hush page to Ansel.

The bell tolls again, and the rooks go up off the roof.

The last line of the row is the acting statement of chapter 26, with a when suffix so that he hands the page over once. Ask a second time and he says his piece again and has nothing to give.

27.1.3 A subject nobody has

A subject with no row gets the platform’s stock answer.

> ask hobb about the weather
Hobb says, "I don't know anything about that."

That line is a standard message, and its alias is asking-unknown-topic. Chapter 22 showed how to replace one. The Relighting adds it to the override messages block it already has.

override messages en-US
  asking-unknown-topic:
    {capitalize the target} gives that some thought, and gives it back.
> ask hobb about the weather
Hobb gives that some thought, and gives it back.

The override serves every person in the story. A table has no otherwise row of its own.

ask hobb for the kettle is a different action from asking about a subject. It is a request for a thing, and with nothing written for it the platform answers “Hobb doesn’t seem inclined to hand over the kettle.”

27.2 Looking things up

A topic table may also belong to a thing that is readable. The player does not ask a book. The player consults it.

define topics for the grimoire
  about "gnusto":
    phrase grimoire-on-gnusto
  about "spark", "sparking", "flame":
    phrase grimoire-on-spark
end topics
> consult grimoire about gnusto
You turn to the margin again. The note is all there is: came over the water as a working, meaning lost. Whoever wrote it underlined "lost" twice.

> look up cheese in grimoire
You find nothing about that in the grimoire.

The standard grammar has three ways of saying it: consult the grimoire about gnusto, look up gnusto in the grimoire, and read about gnusto in the grimoire. The table is written exactly as a person’s is. A directory, a ledger, a dictionary and an index are all this one construct.

27.3 Greetings

A question that has a row is answered at once. Something has to happen when the player opens a conversation with no such question: talk to hobb, or a first question he has no row for. That is a greeting.

define greetings for Hobb
  first time:
    phrase hobb-greets
  on return:
    phrase hobb-greets-again
end greetings
> talk to hobb
He turns the kettle a quarter round on its hook, to no purpose.

"It's you," says Hobb. He does not say which you.

The first time row is used once. After the player has said goodbye, or has walked away and come back, the next opening uses the on return row.

> goodbye
You say goodbye to Hobb.

> talk to hobb
He looks at the blue flame on the sill before he answers.

"Still here," says Hobb, of one of you.

on return may be narrowed by how long the player was gone: on return, again so soon, on return, after a while, and on return, after days. A plain on return covers whichever of those you have not written.

A person with no greetings block has nothing to say to talk to, and the platform answers “Tamsin Vey doesn’t respond.” Tamsin has a table and no greeting, and that suits her for now.

27.3.1 A person who is being talked to stays

While a conversation is open, the person in it does not go on with a goal. Tamsin Vey, asked about her brass in the Refectory, answers, and does not turn to her heap of door furniture as she did in the last chapter. She takes her rounds up again when the player has left her.

27.3.2 The line a greeting needs

Greetings, and the manner below, belong to a fuller model of a person than a topic table needs. A person who has either block must also have at least one line of that model in their own block. Chapter 29 is about those lines. Until then, one is enough, and the plainest is a mood.

mood calm

Leave it out and the story compiles, and then fails when it is loaded.

Error loading story: `Nell` declares conversation blocks but no character model — scenes need a modeled owner. (line 16)

A topic table alone does not need the line. Tamsin already has the model, because a goal is part of it.

27.4 Manner

A character who answers every question in one flat paragraph is a reference book. People do something before they speak.

define manner for Hobb
  when the tower bell is not tolling:
    beat "He turns the kettle a quarter round on its hook, to no purpose."
    beat "He looks at the blue flame on the sill before he answers."
    beat "He takes his time, of which he has had a great deal."
  when the tower bell is tolling:
    beat "He takes one hand off one ear to hear you, and puts it back."
end manner

A manner is a set of rows, each a condition and one or more beats. A beat is a quoted sentence. When the person greets the player or answers from their table, the platform takes the next beat from a row whose condition holds and prints it as a paragraph of its own, ahead of the line.

The beats of a row are used in turn, so Hobb’s three come round one after another and no answer has the beat of the one before it. You have already seen all three in the transcripts above. A row with one beat prints that beat every time, which is right for a man with his hands over his ears.

A beat is not printed before a refusal or before the stock answer for an unknown subject. Those are not the person speaking in their own voice.

The value of the block is that it is separate from the answers. Hobb has five rows in his table and may have fifteen by the end of the book. None of their phrases needs to mention the kettle.

27.5 Build and try it

Copy relighting.story from the companion folder chapter-27 over your own file. It is the story with this chapter’s changes made, and it holds the topic tables, the greetings and the phrases that the chapter did not print in full. To make the changes by hand instead, work from this list and take the rest from that file. Add carries the hush page and mood calm to Hobb, and take the hush page’s in the Stack 3 line away. Add the greetings, the manner and the three topic tables near the people, the one line to override messages, and the new phrases to the phrases block. The story’s day is also longer from this chapter on, since conversation takes turns: the sequence’s first step is at turn 70 and its second 40 turns later.

sharpee compose relighting.story --check
sharpee play .

Ask Hobb about everything in his table, and about something that is not. Then play on until the bell is tolling, come back to the Lodge, and ask him about the bell.

> read hush page
The third working is hardly a word at all. It is the breath you take before one.

The bell tolls. You have stopped counting.

Under the Hood: where the table goes · Story IR topics, greetings, manner, character

In the compiled IR a topic table is the topics list of the entity that owns it, whether that is a person or a book. Each row has a filter and a body. A quoted row’s filter is of kind text, with a primary spelling and its aliases; the body is the same list of statements a clause has. A person’s greetings and manner are two more keys on the entity, and the mood calm line appears under character, the key that also holds the goals of chapter 26. The standard actions asking, telling and consulting each take the player’s words after about as a topic, and all three look the topic up in the owner’s one table.

From another system: asking about

Inform 10 has the actions of asking someone about something and telling someone about something, and an author answers them with rules such as “Instead of asking Hobb about ‘bell’”, or with a table that has a topic column. TADS 3 gives each character a set of topic objects: AskTopic, TellTopic and AskTellTopic for subjects, DefaultAskTellTopic for a subject that has no response, HelloTopic and ByeTopic for greetings and partings, and ConsultTopic for a book. A Chord topic table is close to the TADS arrangement, written as rows of one block where TADS has an object for each.

27.6 What you have so far

Hobb can be asked about the Dimming, the Lantern, Tamsin Vey, his kettle, the word gnusto and the bell, and when the bell is tolling he gives up the page that quiets it. Tamsin answers three questions. The grimoire can be consulted. The game can still be won, and the walk to the win now goes by way of the Lodge.

What a table cannot do is hold a thread. Every question stands alone, and Hobb has no way to ask the player anything. A bargain needs both, and the unbind page is waiting for one.
