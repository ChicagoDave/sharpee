22 Changing the standard messages

Most of what a player reads in a small story was not written by its author. “Taken.” “Time passes…” “You open the oak press.” Those sentences come with the standard actions, and they are plain on purpose, so that they fit anywhere. This chapter is about making them fit here.

It also covers the phrasebook, which is the tool for the opposite problem: text of your own that should be said one way at one time and another way later.

22.1 Finding a message

Every standard message has an alias, a hyphenated name made of the action and the message. The sharpee command will list them all.

sharpee messages

The list is long, 885 messages at this edition, and it is printed as data for a program to read: one line that gives the count, and then the whole list as a single very long line. Each entry in that line looks like this.

{"id":"if.action.taking.taken","alias":"taking-taken","template":"Taken."}

The alias is what you will write. The template is the text the platform prints, and reading it tells you whether you have found the right message. Appendix F is this list, sorted by action, and it is the place to look things up. To find a message without the appendix, send the list to a file, open the file in an editor, and use the editor’s search for a few words of what the game printed. A search that prints whole matching lines will print the entire list, because the list is one line.

22.2 Overriding one message

Walk into the Undercroft without a light and the platform says this.

> down
It is pitch dark. You are likely to be eaten by a grue.

It is a famous sentence, and it belongs to another country. Nothing lives in the dark under Lanternhall. Searching the list for “grue” finds the alias.

{"id":"if.action.going.too_dark","alias":"going-too-dark","template":"It is pitch dark. {You} {be} likely to be eaten by a grue."}

An override is a block at the top level of the file.

override message going-too-dark
  You are at the foot of the steps, in the dark, and that is all you
  can say for certain. You were a student here, and you remember what
  the Undercroft does to shins.
end override

It opens with override message and the alias, holds the new text, and closes with end override.

> down
You are at the foot of the steps, in the dark, and that is all you can say for certain. You were a student here, and you remember what the Undercroft does to shins.

If you mistype an alias, the compiler knows the list as well as you do.

probe.story:7:1 error [analysis.unknown-message-alias] `taking-takn` is not a standard-action message alias — did you mean `taking-taken`?.

22.3 An override is a phrase

The body of an override is the body of a define phrase block, and the head line takes the same additions. So a standard message may have variants and a strategy.

override message waiting-time-passes, cycling
  You wait. The Hall has had thirty years of practice and is better at
  it than you are.
or
  You wait. Somewhere a shutter knocks, twice, and thinks better of it.
or
  You wait.
end override
> wait
You wait. The Hall has had thirty years of practice and is better at it than you are.

> wait
You wait. Somewhere a shutter knocks, twice, and thinks better of it.

> wait
You wait.

Waiting is a good message to spend this on. A player in a game with a clock will wait often, and “Time passes…” thirty times is thirty reminders that they are playing a program.

22.4 Several at once

When you are replacing a number of messages with one plain text each, a single block holds them all.

override messages en-US
  taking-taken:
    You take it.
  looking-room-dark:
    It is dark, and the dark here is old and has no interest in you.

This is the list form, the counterpart of define phrases. It names its language, and each entry is an alias, a colon and one paragraph. An entry here cannot have variants. A message that needs them gets a block of its own.

> take lamp
You take it.

22.5 The markers in a template

Many templates have markers in them.

{"id":"if.action.lighting.lit","alias":"lighting-lit","template":"{You} {light} {the target}."}
{"id":"if.action.taking.taken_from","alias":"taking-taken-from","template":"{You} {take} {the item} from {the container}."}

{You} is the player, capitalised because it opens the sentence. {light} and {take} are verbs, written so that they will agree with whoever is acting. {the target} and {the item} are the things in the command, by the names the action gives them, and they are the same kind of marker as {the thing} in chapter 20.

Your override may use the markers its template uses.

override message taking-taken-from
  {You} {take} {the item} out of {the container}.
end override

Use them whenever the message is about a thing. An override that says “You take the key out of the press” in so many words will say it of the lamp as well. And keep to the markers the template shows. Each message is given the values it needs and no others, so a marker borrowed from a different message has nothing to fill it.

22.6 How much to override

A new author either leaves every standard message alone or sets out to replace all 885. Neither is right.

Replace the messages your players will see most: taking, dropping, waiting, the two darkness messages, the refusal for a direction with no exit. Replace any message whose tone is wrong for your story, as the grue was. Then stop, play the game through, and replace what still jars. A story usually settles at a few dozen overrides, and the rest of the list goes unread because the player never does the thing that prints it.

Before you override a message to change what one thing says, ask whether that thing should say it. The kettle’s refusal is a clause on the kettle, and taking-taken was left alone for it. An override changes a sentence everywhere. A clause changes it for one thing.

22.7 Phrasebooks

Now the opposite problem. Two of the story’s own refusals should change once the Hall begins to wake. While it sleeps, the grille “does not so much as rattle”. Once it is waking, the iron should feel alive. The same goes for Hobb and his kettle.

You could put a when suffix on every refuse, except that refuse does not take one. You could split each key in two and choose between them in every clause that uses them. A phrasebook does it without touching a clause.

A phrasebook is a named set of phrases with one condition on the whole set.

define phrasebook hall-waking while the story is not asleep
  kettle-is-hobbs:
    "Still mine," says Hobb, with his eye on the ceiling.
  grille-fixed:
    It hums under your hand, very faintly, like a rail with a train a
    long way off. It does not move.
end phrasebook

define phrasebook hall-asleep
  kettle-is-hobbs:
    Hobb does not look round. "That's mine," he says.
  grille-fixed:
    It does not so much as rattle.
end phrasebook

The two books hold the same keys. When a clause asks for grille-fixed, the story looks through the phrasebooks in the order they appear in the file and takes the entry from the first book whose condition holds. A book with no while always holds, so it is the book of last resort.

> push grille
It does not so much as rattle.

After the spark page has been read:

> push grille
It hums under your hand, very faintly, like a rail with a train a long way off. It does not move.

The tower bell strikes once, and the sound goes through the stones under your feet.

The grille’s clause still says refuse grille-fixed, as it has since chapter 14.

Three rules keep phrasebooks working.

Order decides. Put the conditional books first and the book with no condition last. If hall-asleep stood first it would always be chosen, since it always holds, and the waking book would never be reached.

A key lives in the books or outside them. A key declared by define phrases or define phrase always wins over a phrasebook. When you move a key into phrasebooks, take its entry out of the define phrases block.

Books are for your keys. An entry in a phrasebook does not change a standard message. Those are changed by override message, and an override has no condition.

An entry may have variants and a strategy, written as in the list form with the strategy after the key: kettle-is-hobbs, cycling: and then the variants with or between them. What it may not have is a while of its own. The condition belongs to the book.

22.8 When to reach for a phrasebook

A phrasebook is the right tool when a mood shifts and several pieces of text should shift with it: before and after the storm, sober and drunk, the Hall asleep and the Hall awake. One line turns the whole set over.

It is the wrong tool for a single thing in a single state, which is a detail phrase or a select on. And it is easy to overuse. Each book you add is another place a reader has to look to find out what a key says. Two or three books is a design. Ten is a puzzle.

22.9 Phrases in a file of their own

A story’s text can outgrow one file. Phrasebooks may be kept in a second file and brought in with one line.

import "books"

The name in quotes is a file beside the story, books.chord. The extension is left off. That file holds define phrasebook blocks and comments and nothing else, and it is not a story by itself: it has no header and cannot be built alone. Its books take their place in the order at the point where the import line stands.

A project has one .story file. Everything else it imports is a .chord file, and you will meet a second use for one in chapter 25.

22.10 Build and try it

Copy relighting.story from the companion folder chapter-22 over your own file. It is the story with this chapter’s changes made. To make the changes by hand instead, work from this list and take the rest from that file: add the override for going-too-dark, the override for waiting-time-passes, the override messages block and the two phrasebooks, and remove kettle-is-hobbs and grille-fixed from the define phrases block.

sharpee compose relighting.story --check
sharpee play .

Go down into the Undercroft without the lamp, and look.

> look
It is dark, and the dark here is old and has no interest in you.

Under the Hood: ids and aliases · if.action.going.too_dark

Each entry of sharpee messages has an id beside the alias. The id is the platform’s own name for the message, and it shows how the platform is arranged: if.action, then the action, then the message. The alias is the same name made safe for Chord, where a key may not contain a dot. In the compiled IR your overrides are stored under messageOverrides by alias, and your phrasebooks under phrasebooks, each with its condition and its entries.

From another system: library messages

Inform 10 keeps its standard replies as lettered “responses” attached to rules, and you change one with a sentence such as: The can’t take yourself rule response (A) is “…”. TADS 3 keeps them in message objects, playerActionMessages and its relatives, which you modify. Chord’s aliases are the same idea with names in place of rule letters. Neither system has a construct quite like the phrasebook. In both you would test a condition inside the message itself.

22.11 What you have so far

Lanternhall speaks with one voice. Its darkness is its own, waiting has three things to say, and the Hall sounds different once it has begun to wake. Every command a player can type is still one the platform supplied, though, and this is a story about four words that are not in any standard list. The next chapter adds verbs.
