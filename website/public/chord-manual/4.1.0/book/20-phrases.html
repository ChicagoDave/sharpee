20 Phrases

Every sentence a player reads in a Chord story is a phrase: a piece of text with a key. You have been writing them since chapter 12, in the one form that chapter needed, a key and its text in the define phrases block.

A phrase can be more than that. It can have several versions and a rule for choosing between them. It can keep its line breaks. It can leave a gap to be filled when it is printed, and it can be dropped into a room’s description and appear there only when it is true. This chapter covers all four.

20.1 Two ways to declare a phrase

The block you know is a list.

define phrases en-US
  stair-sealed:
    The grille stands across the stair, and there is no way round it.
  over-the-parapet:
    You step up onto the parapet to see the court. The court comes up to
    meet you.

Each entry is one key and one paragraph. A story has one such block for each language it is written in, and en-US names the language. Most of your phrases will live here for the whole life of the story.

The second way declares one phrase in a block of its own.

define phrase gate-motto
  The craft needs a light to come home to.
end phrase

It opens with define phrase and the key, and closes with end phrase. For a plain phrase the two ways are equal. The block form exists because its head line has room for more.

A key belongs to one phrase. Declaring the same key twice, in either form or one of each, is an error.

20.2 Variants and strategies

Three things in The Relighting refuse with the same key, no-such-word: the lamp, the candles and the Lantern. A player without the first working will read that sentence several times. Give it three versions.

define phrase no-such-word, cycling
  You reach for the word and find only the place where it was.
or
  The shape of it is there, as a name is on the tip of the tongue. The
  word is not.
or
  Nothing comes. You would need to read it first.
end phrase

The versions are variants, separated by a line that says or. The word after the comma on the head line is the strategy, and the five strategies are the ones chapter 17 listed for select: cycling, stopping, randomly, sticky and first-time.

> light lamp
You reach for the word and find only the place where it was.

> light lamp
The shape of it is there, as a name is on the tip of the tongue. The word is not.

> light lamp
Nothing comes. You would need to read it first.

No clause had to change. The three things still say no-such-word, and the phrase has become richer underneath them. Notice also that the cycle is shared. If the player tries the lamp, then the candles, then the lamp again, they get the first, second and third variants, because it is one phrase keeping one place.

This is the short way promised in chapter 17. When all you want is to vary some text, put the variants in the phrase. Keep select with a strategy for alternatives that do different things.

Remove the entry for no-such-word from the define phrases block when you add this one, or the key is declared twice.

20.3 The sayings of Hobb

The people of this country have a word they say about sunlight. They say it on opening the shutters and on parting, and they say it of anyone who is all cleverness and no warmth. The word is gnusto. Hobb says it the way other people clear their throats.

define phrase hobb-saying, cycling
  "Gnusto on the sill," says Hobb, without turning round. It is what
  people here say for good morning.
or
  "Gnusto keeps what the dark forgets," Hobb remarks, to the kettle.
or
  "All lamp and no gnusto, some people," says Hobb. He does not say
  which people.
end phrase

He says one each time Ansel comes into the Lodge, unless the bell is tolling, in which case nobody can hear him.

create the Lodge, a room
  in the Grounds
  north to the Gate Court

  room name:
    The Porter's Lodge

  The porter's lodge is one small room built into the thickness of the
  gate arch. A chair, a kettle and thirty years of newspapers have filled
  most of it. On the sill a lamp is burning with a steady blue flame, and
  an oak press hangs on the wall beside it. The door to the court is
  north.

  after the player entering
    phrase hobb-saying when the tower bell is not tolling
    phrase hobb-deafened when the tower bell is tolling
  end after
> south
The Porter's Lodge
The porter's lodge is one small room built into the thickness of the gate arch. A chair, a kettle and thirty years of newspapers have filled most of it. On the sill a lamp is burning with a steady blue flame, and an oak press hangs on the wall beside it. The door to the court is north.

You can see a kettle and Hobb here.

On the chair you see a seed cake and a cup.

"Gnusto keeps what the dark forgets," Hobb remarks, to the kettle.

20.4 Text that keeps its shape

Chord treats the text of a phrase as a paragraph. It joins your lines into one and lets the player’s screen wrap them. That is right for prose and wrong for anything whose line breaks matter, such as an inscription.

The letters cut over the gate of Lanternhall are in three lines, centred.

define phrase gate-motto, verbatim
        THE CRAFT
     NEEDS A LIGHT
    TO COME HOME TO
end phrase

verbatim takes the place of a strategy. A verbatim phrase is printed line for line, and the indentation of each line relative to the others is kept. It has one variant and takes no or.

create the gate
  aka gates, arch, letters, motto
  a thing, scenery
  in the Lodge and the Gate Court

  The great gate of Lanternhall, shut for thirty years and opened this
  morning by one leaf. There is a line of cut letters over the arch.

  after the player examining
    phrase gate-motto
  end after
> examine gate
The great gate of Lanternhall, shut for thirty years and opened this morning by one leaf. There is a line of cut letters over the arch.

    THE CRAFT
 NEEDS A LIGHT
TO COME HOME TO

The line that sits furthest left in the file sits at the margin on the screen, and the others keep their distance from it.

20.5 Markers

A marker is a pair of braces in the text of a phrase, with something inside that is filled in when the phrase is printed.

The simplest marker is {br}, which is a line break inside an ordinary paragraph.

The most useful markers take a value handed over by the clause. The phrase statement can pass one, with the word with, a name for it, an equals sign and the value.

create the tower bell
  aka bell
  a thing, scenery
  in the Bell Stair
  states: unrung, tolling, silent

  You cannot see the bell from here, only the dark it hangs in.

  after the player examining
    phrase bell-count with count = tolls when the tower bell is tolling
  end after
define phrases en-US
  bell-count:
    By your count that makes {verbatim:count}, and it is not tired.

The statement passes the counter tolls under the name count, and the marker {verbatim:count} prints it as it stands.

> examine bell
You cannot see the bell from here, only the dark it hangs in.

By your count that makes 3, and it is not tired.

The tower bell tolls.

When the value is a thing, the marker can ask for its name in the form the sentence needs.

Marker Prints
{the thing} the lamp
{a thing} a lamp
{capitalize the thing} The lamp
{verbatim:count} a number or a piece of text, unchanged

Here thing and count are the names given after with. The statement that goes with the first three would be phrase named with thing = the hand lamp.

Markers of this kind matter most in a phrase that several things share, and you will use them in earnest in chapter 23, where one refusal has to name whichever thing the player typed.

One caution is needed. A marker with a plain lower-case word and nothing else, such as {count}, is not read as a value. It is read as the key of another phrase, which is the subject of the next section, and the compiler will tell you that no such phrase exists.

20.6 A phrase inside a description

A room’s description is fixed text, and by chapter 19 that had become a problem. The Bell Stair’s description says that an iron grille stands across the third step. One day the grille will be gone, and the description will go on saying so.

A marker in a room’s description may name a phrase. The phrase’s text is set into the description at that point.

create the Bell Stair, a room
  in the Hall
  up to the Lantern Gallery
  up is blocked while the iron grille is standing: stair-sealed

  The foot of the tower. A stair winds up around the well where the bell
  rope hangs. The cloister is south. {grille-across}

And the phrase can carry a condition on its head line.

define phrase grille-across while the iron grille is standing
  An iron grille stands across its third step.
end phrase

While the grille is standing, the room reads as it always did.

> north
Bell Stair
The foot of the tower. A stair winds up around the well where the bell rope hangs. The cloister is south. An iron grille stands across its third step.

When the grille is lifted, the sentence is simply absent, and nothing in the room’s block had to know.

A phrase set into a description may have variants and a strategy as well, and then the description changes a little each time the player looks.

There are three things to know about this form.

It works in the description of a room. A marker in the description of a thing is not filled in. For a thing, the tool is the detail phrase of the next chapter.

The while on a phrase’s head governs its appearance in a description. It does not stop a clause from printing the phrase with a phrase statement. To make a statement conditional, use the when suffix, as the Lodge’s clause does.

Put the marker at the end of a paragraph when you can. A marker in the middle leaves its two spaces behind when the phrase is absent.

20.7 Build and try it

Copy relighting.story from the companion folder chapter-20 over your own file. It is the story with this chapter’s changes made. To make the changes by hand instead, work from this list and take the rest from that file. This is the first chapter of Part IV, so raise story-version to 0.4.0. Add the four define phrase blocks near the top of the file and remove no-such-word from the define phrases block. Add the clauses to the Lodge, the gate and the tower bell, change the gate’s description, put the marker in the Bell Stair’s description, and add the two new entries bell-count and hobb-deafened.

sharpee compose relighting.story --check
sharpee play .

Walk in and out of the Lodge three times to hear all of Hobb’s sayings. Then read the page, wait for the bell, and go back.

> south

You can see a kettle and Hobb here.

Under the Hood: one table of phrases · Story IR phrases

Both ways of declaring a phrase end up in one table in the compiled IR, keyed by language and then by phrase key. Each entry has a strategy, which is empty for a plain phrase, and a list of variants, each with its text and the markers found in it. Descriptions are in the same table under keys the compiler makes up, such as spark-page.description, which is why a description and a phrase can be treated so alike.

From another system: text substitutions

Inform 10 puts substitutions in square brackets inside the text, and “[the noun]” and “[The noun]” do what {the thing} and {capitalize the thing} do here. TADS 3 uses “{the dobj/him}” style parameters in message strings, and <<expression>> to embed a value. The idea is common to all three. Chord differs in keeping its text out of the code entirely: a clause names a key, and the words are somewhere else, where a writer or a translator can work on them without reading a single clause.

20.8 What you have so far

Phrases can vary, keep their shape, take values and appear in a room’s description only while they are true. All of that is about text the story prints when something happens. The next chapter is about the words a thing carries around with it.
