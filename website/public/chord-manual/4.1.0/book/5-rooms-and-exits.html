5 Rooms and exits

One room is a place to stand. A world is rooms with ways between them. In this chapter The Relighting grows from the Lodge to all eight rooms of Lanternhall, and by the end of it you can walk from the gate to the roof.

5.1 What create does

Everything in a story’s world is made with a create block. The header line gives the thing its name, and the first fact beneath it says what kind of thing it is.

create the Lodge
  a room

The first fact is so nearly universal that Chord lets you fold it onto the header line, after a comma. This book writes rooms that way from here on.

create the Lodge, a room

Every create block names exactly one kind, and the kind comes first. If you leave it out, as in a block that begins create the Refectory and never says what the Refectory is, the compiler lists the kinds there are:

relighting.story:54:1 error [analysis.missing-kind-noun] `Refectory` names no kind. Begin its block with one: `a room`, `a door`, `a person`, `a container`, `a supporter`, `a region`, or `a thing` for anything that is not a room, door, person, container, supporter or region.

That list is most of Part II. You have met a room and a person. Chapter 6 covers a thing, chapter 7 a container and a supporter, chapter 8 a door and chapter 11 a region.

The name is yours to choose. Write it as you want the player to read it, with the in front if it is the kind of name that takes one. The player sees the name of a room as the heading over its description.

5.2 Exits

An exit is a way out of a room. It is one line in the room’s block: a direction, the word to, and the room it leads to.

create the Lodge, a room
  north to the Gate Court

  The porter's lodge is one small room built into the thickness of the
  gate arch. A chair, a kettle and thirty years of newspapers have filled
  most of it. On the sill a lamp is burning with a steady blue flame. The
  door to the court is north.

The exit line goes with the short facts, above the blank line. The room it names does not have to be created yet. It only has to be created somewhere in the file.

There are twelve directions: north, south, east and west, the four between them (northeast, northwest, southeast, southwest), and up, down, in and out.

Notice the sentence added to the end of the Lodge’s description: “The door to the court is north.” The story does not list a room’s exits for the player. Telling the player which ways lead out is the description’s job, and a room whose description forgets to do it is a room the player cannot leave except by trying every direction.

5.3 The way back is free

An exit works in both directions. Because the Lodge has north to the Gate Court, the player can also go south from the Gate Court to the Lodge. You do not write the second line.

You may write it if it makes the file easier to read, as long as it agrees with the first. The Gate Court does so, and it also uses the second way of writing exits:

create the Gate Court, a room
  exits: south to the Lodge, north to the Cloister

The exits: line holds several exits on one line, separated by commas. It means exactly what the same exits would mean on separate lines. Use whichever reads better for the room in hand.

What you may not do is contradict yourself. A room has one exit in each direction, and the free way back counts. Suppose the Den goes up to the Attic, and the Attic then says down to the Cellar. The compiler objects, and its message is one of the most helpful in the language:

relighting.story:12:3 error [analysis.duplicate-exit] `Attic`'s `down` exit leads to `Cellar`, but `Den`'s `up to the Attic` (line 7) also makes `down` from `Attic` lead back to `Den`. If that line should not lead back, write it `up to the Attic, one-way`.

5.4 One-way exits

That message names the remedy. An exit followed by , one-way has no way back.

create the Den, a room
  up to the Attic, one-way

The player can climb from the Den to the Attic and cannot climb down again by that route. If the Attic has no other exit, going down from it prints the platform’s line for a room with no ways out at all:

> down
There are no obvious exits.

On an exits: line, one-way goes before the colon and applies to every exit on the line, as in exits, one-way: up to the Attic.

The Relighting has no one-way exits yet. Use them sparingly. A player who cannot go back the way they came should be able to tell why from the prose.

5.5 The rest of Lanternhall

Here are the six rooms that complete the map. Read the exit lines against the descriptions, and notice that only one room of each connected pair declares the way between them.

create the Cloister, a room
  east to the Refectory
  west to the Library
  north to the Bell Stair
  down to the Undercroft

  A covered walk around a garth gone to nettles. The refectory is east,
  the library west, and the foot of the bell stair north. Worn steps go
  down under the hall.

create the Refectory, a room

  Three long tables and a hearth you could stable a horse in. The
  benches are stacked against the wall, where someone left them for the
  holidays. The cloister is back to the west.

create the Library, a room

  The reading room of Lanternhall, with its desks in rows and its
  galleries of shelves. The stacks run back to the north, out of the
  light. The cloister is east.

create the Undercroft, a room

  Low vaults on squat pillars, running off under the whole of the hall.
  The air is cold and smells of stone. The steps go back up.

create the Bell Stair, a room
  up to the Lantern Gallery

  The foot of the tower. A stair winds up around the well where the bell
  rope hangs. The cloister is south.

create the Lantern Gallery, a room

  The roof of the tower, open on every side. The Great Lantern stands in
  the middle of it on a stone drum, taller than you are, its glass
  thirty years dirty. The stair goes back down.

The Cloister is the hub, so it carries four exit lines and the rooms around it carry none. That is a matter of taste. Putting the exits on the hub keeps the whole map of the Hall in one place in the file.

5.6 The first visit

A room may say something different the first time the player walks into it. The Gate Court does.

create the Gate Court, a room
  exits: south to the Lodge, north to the Cloister

  A square of worn flagstones between the gate and the cloister arch.
  Grass has come up through the joints and been left to get on with it.
  Over the gate, a line of cut letters says that the craft needs a light
  to come home to.

  first time
    You crossed this court every morning for a term, late, with your
    books under your arm. It was larger then. The cloister arch is
    still north of you.

The first time block comes after the description, and its text is indented one level further beneath it. On the first visit the player reads that text in place of the description. On every later visit, and whenever they type look, they read the description.

> north
Gate Court
You crossed this court every morning for a term, late, with your books under your arm. It was larger then. The cloister arch is still north of you.

> look
Gate Court
A square of worn flagstones between the gate and the cloister arch. Grass has come up through the joints and been left to get on with it. Over the gate, a line of cut letters says that the craft needs a light to come home to.

Because the first-visit text replaces the description, anything the player needs from the description has to be in both. That is why the first-visit text ends by saying where the cloister arch is. Without that sentence, a player walking into the Gate Court for the first time would be told nothing about the way north until they typed look.

5.7 A heading of its own

The name you give a room in its create line is the name you use for it everywhere in the file, so a short one is convenient. It is also, by default, the heading the player reads, and there you may want something fuller. A room name block gives the room a heading that is separate from its name.

create the Lodge, a room
  north to the Gate Court

  room name:
    The Porter's Lodge

You go on writing the Lodge in your own lines, and the player reads:

> south
The Porter's Lodge
The porter's lodge is one small room built into the thickness of the gate arch. A chair, a kettle and thirty years of newspapers have filled most of it. On the sill a lamp is burning with a steady blue flame. The door to the court is north.

Without the block, the heading is the room’s name with its the removed, which is why the other rooms read “Gate Court” and “Cloister”.

5.8 Build and try it

From this chapter on, the story is too long to type from the page, and the chapters do not print every line of it. Copy relighting.story from the companion folder chapter-05 over your own file. It is the story with this chapter’s changes made, and it holds the descriptions the chapter did not print in full. “How to read this book” says where the companion folders are.

To make the changes by hand instead, work from this list and take the rest from that file: add the seven new rooms and the changes to the Lodge, and raise story-version: to 0.2.0, since this is the first chapter of Part II. Check the story, then play it.

sharpee compose relighting.story --check
sharpee play .

Walk the whole map. From the Lodge, the commands north, north, north and up take you to the roof.

> north
Bell Stair
The foot of the tower. A stair winds up around the well where the bell rope hangs. The cloister is south.

> up
Lantern Gallery
The roof of the tower, open on every side. The Great Lantern stands in the middle of it on a stone drum, taller than you are, its glass thirty years dirty. The stair goes back down.

Then try a direction that has no exit. On the roof, where the only way is down, try east.

> east
You can't go that way.

If a room you expected is unreachable, the usual cause is a misspelled name on an exit line. The compiler catches that, and guesses what you meant:

relighting.story:30:39 error [analysis.unknown-entity] No entity named `Cloyster` — did you mean `cloister`?.

Under the Hood: the going action · if.action.going

Typing a direction runs one of the platform’s standard actions, called going. An exit line gives that action somewhere to go. Each of the twelve directions is a word the action already understands, along with its short form, such as n for north. You did not define any of that. The directions are a closed list in the language. Chapter 12 shows the ways an exit can be closed, hidden or made dangerous, and all of them work by giving going a reason to refuse.

From another system: the map

Inform 10 builds its map from sentences such as “The Gate Court is north of the Lodge”, and it also makes the way back for you unless you say otherwise. TADS 3 gives each room a property for each direction, and there you write both directions yourself. Chord sits with Inform 10 on this point: one line makes a two-way connection, and you ask for one-way when you want it. Where Chord differs from both is that a room’s exits are lines inside that room’s own block. There is no separate place where the map is described.

5.9 What you have so far

Lanternhall has its eight rooms, and every one of them can be reached. They are empty. The descriptions promise a lamp, a kettle, a gate, a hearth and a Great Lantern, and not one of them exists. The next chapter starts keeping those promises.
