26 People who act

Hobb has been in the Lodge since chapter 6. He is a person, he is proper, and he has a description and a sentence of his own in the room. In every way that matters to the player he is furniture. He never moves, he never does anything, and the only verb he answers is give.

This chapter makes people do things. It covers four tools, in rising order of effort: the pronouns line, which lets your prose refer to a person correctly; the stock behaviours, which move a person about with one word; the acting statement, which has a person perform one action when you say so; and the goal block, which gives a person a plan to carry out on their own turns.

26.1 Pronouns

A person takes one line that a thing does not.

create Hobb
  a person, proper
  pronouns he
  in the Lodge

The pronouns line names a pronoun set. Four are standard: he, she, it and they. The line is allowed only in a person’s block.

What the line buys you is a marker. Chapter 20 showed that a phrase can take a parameter and print it with {the who}. Once a person has been named in a phrase, {pronoun:subject} prints the right pronoun for whoever that was.

define phrases en-US
  own-business:
    {the who} does not stop for you. {capitalize pronoun:subject} has
    {pronoun:possessive} own day to get through.

One phrase now serves anybody. The person is passed in where the phrase is used.

after the player examining
  phrase own-business with who = Tamsin Vey
end after
> examine tamsin
A broad woman in a leather apron, with a pry bar through her belt and brass filings in the creases of her hands. She has the look of someone adding you up.

Tamsin Vey does not stop for you. She has her own day to get through.

A pronoun marker refers to the person most recently mentioned in the same phrase, which is why {the who} comes first. There are five cases, and each has a marker.

Marker For he For she For it For they
{pronoun:subject} he she it they
{pronoun:object} him her it them
{pronoun:possessive} his her its their
{pronoun:possessive-pronoun} his hers its theirs
{pronoun:reflexive} himself herself itself themselves

Put capitalize in front of a marker that begins a sentence. A person with no pronouns line is spoken of as it, or as they if the person is plural.

26.1.1 A set of your own

If the four standard sets do not suit a character, define a fifth.

define pronouns ey
  subject ey
  object em
  possessive eir
  possessive-pronoun eirs
  reflexive emself
end pronouns

create Sam
  a person, proper
  pronouns ey
  in the Yard

The block names the set and gives all five cases, one to a line, in any order. Leaving one out is an error. The name may not be one of the four standard names.

> examine sam
Sam.

Sam looks back. Ey folds eir arms and keeps the moment to emself.

The Relighting does not need a set of its own. Hobb is he, Tamsin Vey is she, and the third person in this chapter is it.

26.2 The stock behaviours

The quickest way to make a person move is one word on the kind line. The platform ships five behaviours, and each is a word you compose exactly as you compose wearable or openable.

The Relighting has a creature for the simplest of them: a scrap of returning magic that takes a liking to Ansel.

create the wisp
  aka light
  a person, follower
  pronouns it

  A scrap of light the size of a thumbnail, the same blue as the lamp
  flame. It keeps a little behind your shoulder, where a dog would.

A follower goes where the player goes. The wisp has no placement line, so it begins the game nowhere, and the hand lamp brings it on the first time the lamp is sparked.

after the player sparking, once
  move the wisp here
  phrase wisp-arrives
end after
> light lamp
You say the word. A flame stands up on the wick, blue at first, as if it had been waiting there with its coat on.

Something small and blue comes loose from the flame and hangs in the air beside it, undecided. Then it decides.

Somewhere above you, old timber takes a weight it had forgotten.

> east
Cloister
A covered walk around a garth gone to nettles. The refectory is east, the library door west, and the foot of the bell stair north. Worn steps go down into the dark under the hall.

A slow draught moves through the Hall, one way and then the other.

The wisp follows you.

The line “The wisp follows you.” is the platform’s. From here to the end of the game the wisp is in the room with Ansel, and a look lists it. On the turn Ansel walks into a room, the room’s list is printed before the wisp arrives, so the wisp is not in that one.

A person needs the word person. The wisp is not much of a person, but it moves by itself and will later hear things, and those are what the kind is for.

26.2.1 The five words

Word What the person does
follower Goes where the player goes, arriving on the same turn.
wanderer Moves to a neighbouring room at random.
patrol Walks a fixed route of rooms.
guard Stays where it is, and says so when the player walks in.
passive Stays where it is and does nothing.

A behaviour word takes settings, written with with and joined by and, as the settings of chapter 9 were.

create the cat
  a person, wanderer with move-chance 50 and allowed-rooms [the Yard, the Shed]
  in the Yard

  A grey cat.

create the watchman
  a person, patrol with route [the Yard, the Shed] and loop true and wait-turns 1 and announces-movement true
  pronouns he
  in the Yard

  A watchman.

A list of rooms is written in square brackets, with commas. A setting that is true or false takes the word true or false.

Setting On Meaning
move-chance wanderer The chance of moving each turn, as a number from 0 to 100.
route patrol The rooms to walk, in order.
loop patrol Whether to begin the route again at its end.
wait-turns patrol How many turns to stand in each room.
allowed-rooms all five The only rooms the person may enter.
forbidden-rooms all five Rooms the person may never enter.
can-move all five Set to false, it holds the person still.
announces-movement all five Whether the player is told of comings and goings.

The last one matters more than it looks. Without it a person who moves is simply in the room or not in it, and the player learns of the move from the room’s list. With it, the platform reports each move the player can see.

> look
Yard
A yard.

You can see a watchman here.

The watchman leaves to the north.

Two of the behaviours speak when the player arrives. A wanderer “notices you”, and a guard “blocks your way!”. The guard’s line is only a line: the player has already walked in. A guard that really stops someone is a blocked exit, which is chapter 12.

26.3 Having a person do something

The stock behaviours move people. To have a person do something, write a sentence with the person as its subject.

on every turn while the oak press is open and the library key is not in the oak press
  Hobb closes the oak press
end on

Hobb closes the oak press is the acting statement. It begins with a person’s name, and the rest is a command exactly as a player would type it, with the verb in the third person. When the statement runs, Hobb performs the closing action. It is the same action the player’s close press reaches, with the same checks, and the platform reports it.

> take key
You take the library key from the oak press.

Hobb closes the oak press.

> open press
You open the oak press, which is empty.

Hobb closes the oak press.

Hobb is a porter. He has spent thirty years shutting things that other people left open, and now the story says so in one line.

Three rules govern the statement.

The words after the name must be a command the story understands. Any standard action will do, and so will an action of your own from chapter 23: Hobb opens the oak press, Nell takes the coin from the box. A verb that no grammar has is an error when you build.

The person performs the action and does not merely have its result. This is the difference between move and acting, and it is worth a moment. move the library key to Hobb puts the key in his hands by decree, from anywhere, with no report. An acting statement has the person do the thing as a player would, under the action’s own checks, and the platform tells the player it was done.

The statement is not for the player, whose actions are the player’s to type.

probe.story:23:5 error [analysis.act-player-actor] `the player takes the bucket` — the player cannot be made to act; an acting statement names a character. Write the character's own name, or use `move` for an authorial change.

It also cannot stand in an on the player … clause, which runs before an action and exists to permit or refuse it. Write it in an after clause, in on every turn, in a when clause, or in the conversation rows of the next two chapters. A when suffix is allowed, as on most statements.

26.4 Goals

An acting statement is one action at a moment you choose. A person who goes somewhere, does three things in order and then stops needs a plan, and a plan is a goal block.

The Relighting’s second person is a salvager.

create Tamsin Vey
  aka salvager, woman
  a person, proper, passive with announces-movement true
  pronouns she
  in the Refectory

  A broad woman in a leather apron, with a pry bar through her belt and
  brass filings in the creases of her hands. She has the look of someone
  adding you up.

  goal rounds, low
    wait for Ansel is in the Refectory
    act tamsin-sorts
    move to the Cloister
    act tamsin-measures
    move to the Refectory
  end goal

A goal has a name and a priority, and its body is a list of steps. The person takes one step on each turn, after the player’s own command. This goal waits until Ansel walks in, and then Tamsin goes about her business with an audience.

> east
Refectory
Three long tables, each with its row of candles, and a hearth you could stable a horse in. The benches are stacked against the wall, where someone left them for the holidays. The cloister is back to the west.

You can see Tamsin Vey here.

> examine tamsin
A broad woman in a leather apron, with a pry bar through her belt and brass filings in the creases of her hands. She has the look of someone adding you up.

Tamsin Vey does not stop for you. She has her own day to get through.

Tamsin Vey turns over a heap of door furniture on the end of a table, and puts two hinges in her apron.

> wait
You wait. The Hall has had thirty years of practice and is better at it than you are.

Tamsin Vey leaves to the west.

The line “Tamsin Vey leaves to the west.” is there because of announces-movement. That is the reason she is passive: the word gives her no movement of its own, and it is a place to hang the setting.

26.4.1 The steps

Step What the person does
move to the <room> Walks to the room, one room a turn.
seek the <thing> Walks toward a thing or a person, one room a turn.
wait for <condition> Stays on this step until the condition holds.
act <phrase> Prints the phrase, if the player is there to see.
say <phrase> Prints the phrase as something said. say <phrase> to <person> names the hearer.
acquire the <thing> Waits until the thing is in the room, then takes it.
give the <thing> to <person> Hands it over, waiting if the other person is not there.
drop the <thing> Puts it down.
a command Performs that action, as the acting statement does.

The last row is the general one. Any command the story understands may be a step, written without the person’s name, since the goal’s owner is the one acting: open the box, go east, push the iron grille. If the action is refused, the person tries again on the next turn.

A step has no when suffix. A step that must wait for something is preceded by a wait for step.

Here is a small complete plan, from a story of two rooms.

create Nell
  a person, proper
  pronouns she
  in the Yard

  Nell.

  goal fetch, high
    active when Pat is in the Shed
    move to the Shed
    open the box
    take the coin from the box
    act nell-bites
    wait for Pat is in the Yard
    seek Pat
    say nell-done to Pat
  end goal
> look
Shed
A shed.

You can see a box and Nell here.

Nell opens the box.

In the box you see a coin.

> look
Shed
A shed.

You can see a box and Nell here.

In the box you see a coin.

Nell takes the coin from the box.

> look
Shed
A shed.

You can see a box and Nell here.

Nell bites the coin.

acquire takes a thing that is lying loose in the room. For a thing inside something, write the command, as Nell’s plan does.

A person’s steps happen whether or not the player is watching. Phrases printed by act and say are seen only by a player in the same room, and a goal that runs to its end in an empty room has still run. A goal is finished when its last step is done, and it does not begin again.

26.4.2 When a goal is active

A goal may carry one active when line. The goal is considered only while its condition holds.

goal the-grille, high
  active when the tower bell is tolling
  move to the Bell Stair
  say tamsin-at-the-grille
  push the iron grille
  act tamsin-tries-the-bar
end goal

Tamsin now has two goals. The priority, which is one of critical, high, medium and low, decides between goals that are active at once. While the Hall sleeps only rounds is active. When the bell begins to toll, the-grille becomes active and outranks it, and Tamsin leaves whatever she was doing and walks to the foot of the tower. She takes a step a turn, so what you see depends on when you follow. This is the view two turns after she comes through the Cloister.

> north
Bell Stair
The foot of the tower. A stair winds up around the well where the bell rope hangs. The cloister is south. An iron grille stands across its third step.

You can see Tamsin Vey here.

The tower bell tolls.

The wisp follows you.

Tamsin Vey runs a thumb down one bar of the grille and looks at the thumb. "That," she says, "was not here on Tuesday."

> wait
You wait.

The bell tolls again, and the rooks go up off the roof.

Tamsin Vey pushes the iron grille.

> wait
You wait. The Hall has had thirty years of practice and is better at it than you are.

The bell tolls. You have stopped counting.

Tamsin Vey sets her pry bar to the foot of the grille and leans. The bar bends. The grille does not notice.

The middle line is the platform’s report of a real push. The grille’s own clause from chapter 14 says on the player taking, pushing, pulling, and it concerns the player only, so Tamsin’s push goes through to the standard action and its standard result.

That is the whole of a second character’s morning in fourteen lines, and none of it is a clause on a thing. A goal belongs to the person who has it.

26.5 Build and try it

Copy relighting.story from the companion folder chapter-26 over your own file. It is the story with this chapter’s changes made, and it holds the phrases, which the chapter did not print in full. This is the first chapter of Part V, and the copy’s story-version is 0.5.0. To make the changes by hand instead, work from this list and take the rest from that file: add pronouns he to Hobb, add the on every turn clause to the oak press and the after the player sparking, once clause to the hand lamp, create Tamsin Vey and the wisp near Hobb, and add their six phrases to the phrases block.

sharpee compose relighting.story --check
sharpee play .

Walk to the Refectory and wait a turn or two, then follow Tamsin into the Cloister. Read the spark page, light the lamp, and wait in the Cloister for the bell. Tamsin comes through on her way to the tower. Wait two turns more and then go north, and the Bell Stair reads as it does above. Go sooner or later and the same lines come on other turns.

> wait
You wait.

The tower bell tolls.

Tamsin Vey enters from the east.

Under the Hood: behaviours and plans · Story IR traits, character.goals

In the compiled IR a behaviour word is one more entry in a person’s traits list, beside proper, with its settings in a config list: Tamsin has passive there with announces-movement. The platform turns each into an NPC behaviour that is consulted once a turn. A person’s goals are recorded under character.goals, each with its priority, its activeWhen condition and its steps. A command step such as push the iron grille is stored with the action it resolved to (pushing) and the pattern it matched (push :target), which is why a misspelt command in a goal is a build error and not a silent failure at turn forty. When an NPC acts, it runs the same standard action a player’s command runs.

From another system: actors acting

In Inform 10 an action can be tried by another person, with a phrase such as “try Hobb closing the oak press”, and the action is then subject to the usual action machinery. A person’s routine can be written as “Every turn” rules. In TADS 3 a non-player character’s goals are AgendaItem objects, each with an isReady condition and an invokeItem method that is called when the item is carried out. A Chord goal is nearest to a TADS agenda item, with the difference that its body is a list of steps taken one a turn and not a method.
