25 Bending the standard grammar

A player does not know which words you chose. They will type fix lens where you wrote mend, sit on chair where the platform knows enter, and light lamp where the whole point of your story is that lamps are sparked. A story that answers “I don’t understand that” to a reasonable sentence loses a little of the player’s trust each time.

This chapter is about the grammar of actions that already exist: adding words to them, taking words away, and moving a word from one action to another. It is the shortest chapter in Part IV, and it has a large effect on how a story feels to play.

25.1 Adding words to an action

extend action adds patterns to an action. The action may be one of your own.

extend action mending
  grammar
    fix or repair the target

extend action hushing
  grammar
    quiet or silence the target

The block names the action and holds a grammar block, written as in define action. Now fix, repair and mend are one verb, with one set of requirements and one trait answering it.

> fix spectacles
You say the word and mean it. What was broken is whole, and has no memory of having been otherwise.

You could have written all three words in the action’s own grammar block, and for a new action that is often tidier. extend action earns its place when the action is not yours to edit.

25.2 Extending a standard action

Hobb’s chair has been enterable since chapter 7, and chapter 7 had to admit that the command for sitting in it was enter chair. In the story as chapter 24 left it, the plainer command gets this reply:

> sit on chair
I don't understand that.

The standard action is called entering, and it can be extended like any other.

extend action entering
  grammar
    sit on or in the portal
    sit down on or in the portal

With that in the story, as it is in this chapter’s companion file, the command works.

> sit on chair
You get into the chair.

> exit
You get out of the chair.

The slot is called the portal here because that is what the standard entering action calls it. When you extend a standard action, use its own slot names, so that the rest of the action knows which thing your pattern means. Appendix C gives the patterns of every standard action, and the slot names are there to be read off: the item for taking, the target for lighting, the portal for entering.

The reply still says “get into”, which is the standard message entering-entered. You know from chapter 22 how to change that.

An extend action block adds grammar and nothing else. It cannot add a requirement, a refusal or a statement to the action. Behaviour belongs to things and traits, by the rule of the last two chapters, and the compiler refuses a behaviour line in an extend action block.

25.3 Taking words away

remove from action takes a pattern out of a standard action.

remove from action lighting
  light the target

The block names the action, and each line beneath is a pattern to remove, written exactly as the standard grammar has it. The pattern must be one the action really has. Removing a pattern that is not there is an error and not a silent success, and the message lists the patterns the action does have.

relighting.story:192:1 error [analysis.unmatched-removal-pattern] `remove from action lighting` — no standard rule matches `ignite :target`. The action's standard patterns are: `light :target`, `light :target with|using :tool`.

After this block, light lamp is not a command at all. The standard lighting action still exists, with its other pattern, light the target with the tool. It has lost only the words you named.

By itself that would make the story worse. It is one half of a move.

25.4 Moving a word

The last chapter left a hole and a patch. light lamp reached the standard lighting action, and the wick trait refused it with a sentence about matches. That is a fair answer, and a better one is to let the player be right. In this story, to light a thing is to spark it.

remove from action lighting
  light the target

extend action sparking
  grammar
    light the target

The first block frees the pattern. The second gives it to sparking. Write both. An extend action block is allowed to add a pattern that a standard action also has, and the story would then have two actions claiming one command. Removing the pattern first leaves no doubt about which you mean.

> light lamp
You say the word. A flame stands up on the wick, blue at first, as if it had been waiting there with its coat on.

Somewhere above you, old timber takes a weight it had forgotten.

And before the page has been read, light lamp is refused by the sparking action, in the words the story uses for a working that has not been learned.

> light lamp
You reach for the word and find only the place where it was.

The wick trait keeps its on the player lighting clause. It now guards the one pattern the lighting action has left.

> light lamp with taper
You have no match, and you did not walk here for three days to use one. There is a word for this.

A slow draught moves through the Hall, one way and then the other.

This is the right order in which to think about any standard verb that your story changes the meaning of. First decide what the player will type. Then make sure every reasonable way of typing it arrives at the same action.

25.5 What to add

Play your story as someone who has never seen the source, or better, watch someone else play it. Each time a sensible command is not understood, write it down. Most of the entries on that list are one line in an extend action block.

A few habits help.

Add the plain word beside the coloured one. Your verb is spark, and the player’s is light. Your verb is unbind, and theirs may be open. The Relighting does not give open to unbinding, because the grille has no door in it and “open grille” deserves the standard refusal, but it is the kind of question to ask of every working.

Add the prepositions people actually use. sit on, sit in and sit down on are one pattern each, or one pattern with or in it.

Do not add a synonym that means something else elsewhere in the story. If ring is a noun in your game, think before making it a verb.

And do not take away a standard pattern merely because your story has no use for it. A command that is understood and politely refused is better than one that is not understood.

25.6 A file for the grammar

The synonym blocks are not part of the story’s design. They are a list that grows with testing, and they can live in a file of their own.

import "synonyms"

This is the import line of chapter 22, and the file it names is synonyms.chord, beside the story.

## Plain words for the workings, and a way to sit down. This file is a
## fragment: The Relighting imports it, and it is not a story by itself.

extend action mending
  grammar
    fix or repair the target

extend action hushing
  grammar
    quiet or silence the target

extend action entering
  grammar
    sit on or in the portal
    sit down on or in the portal

The blocks are read as though they stood in the story at the line where the import is. The Relighting keeps the light move in the main file, because that one is a decision about what the story means, and puts the rest in the fragment.

The standard grammar is itself a file of this kind, written in the same define action blocks you learned in chapter 23. Appendix C prints it in full. Reading it is the quickest way to learn what your players can already say.

25.7 Build and try it

Copy relighting.story and synonyms.chord from the companion folder chapter-25 into your project, the first over your own file and the second beside it. They are the story with this chapter’s changes made. To make the changes by hand instead, work from this list: add the remove from action block, the extend action sparking block and the import line near the workings, and create synonyms.chord beside the story with the three blocks above.

sharpee compose relighting.story --check
sharpee play .

Play the winning walk once more with the plain words: light lamp, quiet bell, fix spectacles, repair lens. The grille has no plain word, so unbind grille is typed as before. Leave it until after fix spectacles.

> quiet bell
You say the word up into the dark of the tower. The bell swings twice more without a sound, and hangs still.

One of the replies on this walk ends with the line about the rank of a Journeyman. It is whichever takes the score to thirty, and in the order given here that is fix spectacles.

Under the Hood: two lists · Story IR grammarRemovals, grammarExtensions

The compiled IR records these blocks in two lists. grammarRemovals has one entry, for lighting, holding the pattern that was taken away. grammarExtensions has four, for sparking, mending, hushing and entering, and it does not say which file they came from. When the story loads, the platform starts from the standard grammar, removes the first list, and adds the second and your own actions. The parser that reads the player’s command never sees your source. It sees one grammar, which is the standard one as your story has bent it.

From another system: Understand lines

In Inform 10 the same three jobs are done by “Understand” sentences: “Understand ‘fix [something]’ as mending” adds a pattern, and “Understand the command ‘light’ as something new” clears a verb so that you can assign it again. In TADS 3 you add a VerbRule for the new wording. Chord’s two blocks correspond closely to the two Inform forms. The difference is that Chord removes one pattern at a time, where Inform’s “as something new” clears every use of the word.

25.8 What you have so far

That is Part IV, and with it the game has its centre. The Relighting has four workings, three of which can be typed three or four ways, things that answer them through traits, prose that varies and belongs to its speakers, and a standard library that has been taught this story’s manners. A player can win it, lose it two ways, or run out of day.

What Lanternhall does not have is anybody in it. Hobb stands in his doorway and says a word about sunlight when you come in, and he cannot be asked a single question. Part V is about people.
