35 The opt-in systems

The Relighting’s header has four lines that begin with use.

story
  title: The Relighting
  id: relighting
  use scoring
    rank "Latecomer" at 0
    rank "Second-Year" at 10
    rank "Journeyman" at 30
    rank "Lamplighter" at 50
  use chapters
  use expanses
  use sailing

Each arrived in the chapter that needed it. This chapter is about the line itself, and about two systems the game does not use. Nothing here is added to The Relighting. A story about relighting a lantern does not need a fight in it, and it should not grow one to fill a chapter.

35.1 What use means

Most of Chord is always available. Rooms, things, people, clauses, timers, conversations and machines need no permission.

A few systems are larger than a word. They bring vocabulary of their own, they run something on every turn, and they print lines the author did not write. A story that does not want them should not pay for them or be surprised by them. Those systems are opt-in, and use <name> in the story header is how a story opts in.

A use line does two things. It admits the system’s words, so that the compiler will accept an expanse or combatant. And it tells the platform to start the system when the story loads.

The rules are few. A use line goes in the story header and nowhere else. Each system is named once. All but two are a single line: use scoring carries its rank ladder beneath it, as chapter 18 showed, and use hunger carries its own lines, below. A system may need another, as sailing needs expanses, and the compiler says so.

Using a word without its line is an error that names the line. So is a use line for something that needs none. People and machines are core, and use npc is refused.

Line What it brings Chapter
use scoring score, award, the rank ladder 18
use chapters define chapters, during, before, after 19
use expanses an expanse, landmarks, movement: open 34
use sailing wind:, the sailing block, sails: 34
use combat combatant, weapon, fighting this one
use hunger a hunger meter with rungs this one

35.2 Combat

use combat brings two trait words. A person who can fight is a combatant, and a thing to fight with is a weapon.

story
  title: The Cellar
  id: cellar
  use combat
  authors:
    Nobody

create the Cellar, a room

  A low brick cellar that smells of coal and damp.

create the rat
  a person, combatant with health 3 and skill 20 and base-damage 1
  in the Cellar

  A rat the size of a boot.

create the poker
  a thing, weapon with damage 3
  in the Cellar

  An iron poker.

create Ansel
  a person
  playable
  starts in the Cellar

  You came down for coal.

before the game starts
  change the player to Ansel
end before

That is a whole story, with a room, a player and a start block as Part I taught them. Both words take settings, written with with and and as the behaviour words of chapter 26 do. health is how much damage a combatant can take. skill is how likely its blows are to land, and base-damage is what a blow does bare-handed. A weapon’s damage is what it adds.

The standard attack action has been in the grammar all along. Against something that is not a combatant it does nothing of consequence. Against a combatant it is a fight.

> attack rat
You land a solid blow on the rat, dealing 1 damage!

> take poker
Taken.

> attack rat with poker
You have slain the rat!

An attack may also miss: “You swing at the rat but miss!” Whether it does is a roll against skill. sharpee play rolls afresh each time it starts, so your fight will not go blow for blow as this one did, and the rat may take more attacks or fewer. A test tree is different. It carries a seed, as chapter 38 describes, and the rolls come from that seed, so a tree that pins a fight sees the same fight every time.

All of these lines are standard messages. A story with combat in it will want its own, and chapter 22’s override message is how.

The appendix of trait words lists every setting the two words take. Reach for combat when fighting is part of what your game is. A single guard who must be got past is better written as a blocked exit and a clause, with prose you control, than as a system of numbers that the player meets once.

35.3 Hunger

use hunger gives the player a meter that rises with time.

story
  title: The Yard
  id: yard
  use hunger
    grows 1 each turn
    peckish at 2 says hunger-peckish
    hungry at 4 says hunger-hungry
    fatal at 9
  authors:
    Nobody

create the Yard, a room

  A cobbled yard with a pump in the middle of it.

create Ansel
  a person
  playable
  starts in the Yard

  You have not eaten since yesterday.

before the game starts
  change the player to Ansel
end before

define phrases en-US
  hunger-peckish:
    You could eat.
  hunger-hungry:
    You are hungry now, and thinking about the loaf.

The lines beneath use hunger are its settings. grows <n> each turn is the rate. Each line of the form <name> at <n> is a rung: a level of the meter with a name of your choosing. A rung may end with says and a phrase, and the phrase is printed once, when the meter first reaches the rung. fatal at <n> is the level at which the player dies.

The meter starts at nothing. The opening of a story is not a turn, so the meter still stands at nothing when the player first reads the Yard, and each command after that adds one. The second wait takes it to two.

> wait
Time passes...

> wait
Time passes...

You could eat.

> wait
Time passes...

> wait
Time passes...

You are hungry now, and thinking about the loaf.

Left alone, the meter reaches nine on the ninth wait.

> wait
Time passes...

You have starved to death.

[The story has ended. RESTART, RESTORE or UNDO]

This is the form of chapter 18’s rank ladder, turned upside down. A rank is a threshold on a number that rises as the player does well, and a rung is a threshold on a number that rises as time passes. Both speak once on the way up.

35.3.1 Announcing

A system that crosses thresholds has to decide how much to say about it. A use line may end with , announce and a mode.

use hunger, announce silent

silent prints nothing at a rung. The meter still rises, and the fatal level still ends the game with its line. The other modes are all, which is the default and reports every rung as it is reached, and collapsed and combined, which say less when several rungs are crossed at once.

The suffix is allowed on any use line, and it matters for the ones that count something, which are hunger and scoring.

35.4 Choosing

An opt-in system saves work when its model is the model your game has. Scoring is that for most games, and The Relighting used it without a second thought. Expanses were that for one scene of this game and would have been absurd for the rest.

When a system’s model is nearly what you want, be careful. You will spend the saved work bending it. A meter is a counter, a rung is a comparison, and a starving player is kill the player with a when on it. Chapter 18 gave you all three, and a story that wants a thirst that only matters in one desert can write it in ten lines and own every word.

35.5 Build and try it

There is no companion snapshot for this chapter. The two examples above are complete stories, and you can build them as you built the first story in Part I. Each needs a folder of its own, because a folder holds one story. Make a folder called cellar beside your project, and later one called yard in the same place. Type the first example into a file called cellar.story inside it, and run these two commands there.

sharpee compose cellar.story --check
sharpee play .

Attack the rat, take the poker, and attack again until the rat is dead. Then make a folder called yard, type the second example into yard.story inside it, and do the same.

sharpee compose yard.story --check
sharpee play .

Type wait until the story ends, and count the turns between the lines.

Under the Hood: a manifest for each system · Story IR uses, announceModes

Each opt-in system is described to the compiler by a small manifest: its name, the trait words it adds, and the settings each word takes with their types. That is how weapon with damage three can be an error at build time though the compiler knows nothing about fighting. The compiled IR lists the systems a story opted into under uses, and any announce suffix under announceModes. When the story loads, the platform starts one plugin for each name in uses, and each plugin takes its turn in the cycle after the player’s command.

From another system: extensions

Both Inform 10 and TADS 3 handle this with libraries that a story includes. An Inform story writes “Include”, the name of an extension and its author, as in “Include Rideable Vehicles by Graham Nelson.” A TADS 3 project names its libraries in the project file, with a -lib line for each. A Chord use line is narrower than either. It turns on a system that ships with the platform, and it cannot name code that the story’s author wrote.

35.6 What you have so far

That is Part VI. You have every part of the language that builds a world and makes it move: rooms and things, clauses and conditions, phrases and verbs, people who act and talk and feel, machines, vehicles, open water, and the systems a story may opt into.

Two things remain. The player meets a story through a page, with sound and pictures and a layout, and Part VII is about that page. And a story that you mean other people to play has to be proved and shipped, which is Part VIII.
